## 1. Auditor's Core Flow — Click-Path (Mapped Before Screen Sketches)

This is the click-path the two upcoming screens (content warning, AI analysis summary) sit inside. The outer shape is client-confirmed from the kickoff demo and the sprint-review Q&A: a submitted case gets assigned to an auditor, the auditor passes through a warning gate before viewing flagged content, reviews it, and submits findings to the manager. Where the client left specifics open, this table marks the team's working design intention.

**Reading this as four flows:** the 9 stages below collapse into four flows that serve as a shared mental model for the wider team. This is a plain-language index into the same stages, not a replacement for them — use it for a quick shared picture, and the table below for the actual design detail and citations.
1. **Normal Review** — Stages 1–5: assigned → queue → consent gate → review → submission.
2. **High-Severity Cooldown** — Stages 6–9: submission triggers cooldown → cooldown screen → wellbeing check-in → return to queue.
3. **Decline** (branches off Stage 3) — routes straight to the manager; no SOS, no push alert.
4. **SOS Emergency** (available throughout Stage 4) — immediate stop, on-screen acknowledgement, manager alert/email, then follow-up.

| Stage | Auditor action / thought | Status | Design note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Case assigned | A submitted case is weighted-assigned to the auditor in the background; case shows "AI analysis in progress" while processing runs | Confirmed | Weighting is based on exposure level + case count, round-robin across auditors — not purely random. **Left open:** this weighting balances totals across auditors, but doesn't yet say whether it should also avoid assigning the same auditor several high-severity cases in a row. That's an algorithmic assignment-backend question, not a screen-level UX one — it needs Dev/BA input, and nothing is proposed here to resolve it. |
| 2. Case appears in queue | Once analysis completes, the case appears in the auditor's list with a severity tag; the thumbnail is auto-blurred/suppressed by default | Confirmed | Auditor isn't exposed just by seeing the case list |
| 3. Warning / consent gate | Auditor opens the case; a pop-up shows the AI-identified tags/flag reason and asks whether to proceed; auditor accepts or respectfully declines | Confirmed mechanism, wording/content open | Plain, neutral wording ("Would you like to proceed?", dropping "willingly") per Marielle Lee Q1. **Team-designed:** surface the flag's reason/category, not just a severity tag, before the decision — per Marielle Lee Q2, so declining isn't "a cold, unexplained judgment call." **Team-designed:** the decline path carries a one-line non-judgment statement ("This won't be held against you — your manager reviews the case, not your decision"), per Research Note 1, aimed at Jules's and Maya's fear that declining is silently tracked. A decline routes directly to the manager (confirmed). |
| 4. Review | Auditor watches the video (unblurs on deliberate play), can toggle blur/grayscale, reads the AI severity/CVI score, incident timeline, narrative summary, and audio transcript/timestamps; can trigger SOS if unexpectedly exposed, or a separate wellbeing check-in if a correctly-tagged case is simply difficult | Confirmed core + team-designed | **Team-designed:** an on-screen SOS acknowledgement beyond the confirmed manager-email trigger, giving visible confirmation a human will follow up, not just that an email was sent. **Team-designed:** a persistent breakdown near the exposure counter of what actually counts as "exposed" time (re-watches, blurred-but-open), per Research Note 3 — pre-empts Theo's dominant exposure-clock distrust without a help-doc detour mid-review. Blurred/grayscale/muted time counting as exposure isn't just an internal guess: a 2026 experimental study found blur and grayscale didn't significantly reduce immediate anxiety, negative affect, or intrusive-image effects compared to unmodified content — the controls stay for reviewer autonomy, but shouldn't be assumed to reduce exposure risk. **Team-designed:** a *separate* wellbeing check-in distinct from SOS, per Marielle Lee's own sketch ("Do you feel uncomfortable with this video?" plus a direct talk-to-manager trigger) — kept separate because SOS is confirmed as the response to an AI *failure*/unexpected over-exposure, not to an ordinary hard case; collapsing both into one button would either dilute SOS's urgency or leave the far more common case unsupported. |
| 5. Submission | Auditor can adjust the AI's severity/CVI rating and add comments, then submits to the manager; the case moves into the manager's review queue | Confirmed | Both the AI's and the auditor's input are recorded |
| 6. Cooldown begins (conditional, if the case just submitted triggers it) | The case just submitted triggers the mandatory cooldown; instead of returning to the queue, the auditor lands on a dedicated cooldown screen that states plainly why | Confirmed mechanism, trigger proposed (pending sign-off) | Positioned right after Submission, not as a mid-review interrupt — cooldown responds to exposure already completed, not a system failure mid-watch, which is also why it stays separate from SOS (confirmed response to unexpected over-exposure/AI failure only, not an ordinary high-severity case). **Team-designed:** the screen names the reason in plain, non-punitive terms — e.g. "You've just reviewed a high-severity case. This cooldown is a built-in protection, not a penalty" — per Marielle Lee Q2's reframing that reviewing is a normal part of the job, not something requiring an apology; naming it as protection rather than consequence follows the same non-judgment logic already used in Stage 3's decline copy (Research Note 1). The exact severity level that triggers this now has a sourced proposal — see the Cooldown Severity Model below. |
| 7. During cooldown | Queue and new assignments stay locked; the auditor sees remaining time and today's aggregate exposure status (running total, cases handled) — not a recap of the case just reviewed, and not a blank wait | Team-designed | **Team-designed:** remaining time shows as a real end-time ("Cooldown ends at 2:47pm"), not a bare countdown — extending the same transparency fix already proposed for the exposure clock in Stage 4 (Research Note 3); a number with no visible logic stops reassuring Theo's persona and becomes something to mentally audit instead. Marielle Lee (Q5) treated self-status visibility as clarifying, not pressuring — but that guidance was about *aggregate* progress indicators ("1 of 10 completed," a workload counter), not about recapping the specific disturbing content of the case that triggered the cooldown; those are two different things. This screen shows the aggregate version only — today's running exposure total and case count — which is exactly what Q5 defends, while deliberately excluding a recap of the specific case just reviewed, since re-surfacing the disturbing material would not serve the same clarifying purpose and would sit against the cooldown's calming intent. Generic self-care copy is intentionally not included: no cited source recommends it, and a stock calming message would be the same "timer + calming message" answer this cooldown design is meant to move past. |
| 8. Wellbeing check-in (within cooldown) | A one-line, optional check-in prompt appears — distinct from SOS — with a second, equally optional way to flag "I'd like a break" | Team-designed | **Team-designed:** the general wellbeing flow sketch's proposed check-in surfaces here specifically, not after every case — "Do you want to talk to your manager about this video?", phrased per Marielle Lee's own sketch (Q6) — kept infrequent rather than repeated after every case, since Marielle (Q4) warned that constant visible protective signals read as surveillance rather than care. Answering is optional and carries no urgent/alarm framing, keeping it distinct from SOS, which remains the confirmed response to unexpected over-exposure/AI failure, not to an ordinary high-severity case. The prompt's *appearance* is the mandatory part — it's tied to the confirmed Stage 6 cooldown trigger, not something the auditor has to ask for — while *answering* stays optional, exactly as already stated above. **Team-designed:** alongside "Do you want to talk to your manager," the prompt carries a second, separate optional flag, "I'd like to take a break," kept to the same one-line/one-button register Marielle Lee (Q6) describes — specifically so the far more common "just logging this, no action needed" response doesn't require any manager step at all (see Manager Assumptions Note, Section 2a); only a break request does. This keeps routine check-ins low-friction across a manager's full caseload while giving the one clear, actionable signal manager follow-up should respond to. |
| 9. Cooldown ends | Auditor presses "Return to queue" when ready; the system doesn't auto-redirect the instant the timer hits zero | Team-designed, duration proposed (pending sign-off) | **Team-designed:** ending on a deliberate action rather than an automatic redirect mirrors Stage 3's informational-before-decision structure — "timer done" shouldn't silently become "back to full speed" without the auditor's own signal. **Proposed, not yet decided:** the exact severity trigger (Stage 6) and duration (this stage) now have a sourced proposal rather than no direction at all — see the Cooldown Severity Model below — but neither is client-confirmed or team-ratified yet. |

### Cooldown Severity Model & Duration (Supporting Stages 6 and 9)

The exact severity trigger and cooldown duration were previously fully open — no client ruling and no proposal existed anywhere. There is now a well-sourced proposal to react to, contributed via Jana's research; it is not yet formally adopted by the client or the wider team.

| Level | Exposure severity | Examples | Cooldown |
|---|---|---|---|
| S1 – Low | Disturbing but limited impact | Verbal aggression, non-graphic conflict, staged/synthetic violence with no visible injury | None — normal review |
| S2 – Moderate | Realistic or sustained distress | Real physical conflict, visible minor injury, aggressive audio, repeated distressing content | 5 minutes, after sustained/repeated exposure |
| S3 – High | Graphic or psychologically intense | Serious injury, significant blood, death aftermath, realistic self-harm, sexual violence themes | 15 minutes, mandatory |
| S4 – Critical | Extremely traumatic / specialist material | The most severe content categories the platform handles | 30 minutes minimum, plus a mandatory manager/support check-in and the option to stop or reassign the remaining shift |

Severity is calculated from six weighted factors — content type, realism, graphic detail, duration/repetition, vulnerable context, and sensory intensity — rather than a single violence flag, so a short staged clip and a short real one don't score the same. A minimum-severity floor also applies: the most severe content categories can't be scored below S3/S4 regardless of clip length or the calculated score. After an S3 or S4 case, the next case assigned should not itself be graphic, so cooldown isn't immediately followed by another high-severity case.

**Sourced from:** WorkSafe Victoria's Psychological Health Compliance Code (Appendix C — automated flagging, grayscale/thumbnails, rotating high-risk tasks, wellbeing checks), Safe Work Australia's Model Code of Practice on psychosocial hazards (minimising traumatic-material exposure, role rotation, recovery time), the Australian Classification Board's impact framework (realism, graphic detail, duration/repetition, context), Spence et al. (2024) and DeMarco, Watson & Norman (2026) on dose-response trauma exposure in content moderation, and Albulescu et al.'s (2022) microbreak meta-analysis on recovery-window length for highly demanding tasks.

**Still open:** whether the AI pipeline can reliably compute all six severity factors is a Dev feasibility question, not decided here. And per the source material itself, these exact minute values are a proposed risk-control policy informed by occupational-safety research, not an established clinical standard — stated as such rather than presented as settled science.

Stage 3's exact pop-up copy, Stage 4's SOS/wellbeing-check-in interactions, and Stages 6–9's cooldown experience are genuinely open design space; each team-designed addition above is traceable to a specific research note or Marielle Lee recommendation (cited inline), not invented from scratch — treat this table as the team's working design intention for the two upcoming screens plus the cooldown addition, not something awaiting outside confirmation. Stages 6 and 9's trigger threshold and duration are a different kind of open, though: no client ruling exists for either, and the Cooldown Severity Model above is a proposal, not a confirmation — only what the Auditor experiences once cooldown is triggered is fully team-designed and settled. Separately, whether assignment weighting (Stage 1) should also account for consecutive high-severity assignments to the same auditor is open too — that one sits with Dev/BA, not UX, since it's a backend assignment-logic question.

## 2. Auditor's Likely Background/Context (Brief Note)

An auditor works through a queue of AI-pre-processed cases against a daily, time-measured exposure cap, with blur-by-default and a mandatory cooldown after high-severity exposure (shaped in Stages 6–9 below) — they can also decline a case, or trigger SOS if the AI under-tags something and they're unexpectedly exposed. Per our Week 1 auditor research and the three dedicated Auditor persona variants, this isn't one fixed relationship to those protections but three postures across a tenure spectrum: still forming first impressions, with no track record to judge the protections by (Jules, 3 weeks in, no prior moderation experience); competent, but not yet confident that declining or SOS carries no real professional cost (Maya, ~3 years, the client brief's own named reviewer); and having largely resolved that trust, but sharpened instead into doubt over whether the exposure clock itself is accurate (Theo, ~9 years). For Jules and Maya, that's why Stage 3's decline path carries its own non-judgment reassurance and Stage 4's SOS needs visible human acknowledgement, not just a background email — the same trust gap the Manager Assumptions Note's Hypothesis 1 closes from the manager's side. For Theo, it's why Stage 4's exposure-clock breakdown matters more than reassurance ever would. No real auditors have been interviewed for this project, so this spectrum, like the rest of the Auditor persona set, is a team-defined working hypothesis rather than a confirmed profile.
