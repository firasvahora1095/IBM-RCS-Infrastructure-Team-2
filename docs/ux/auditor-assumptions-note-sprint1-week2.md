## 1. Auditor's Core Flow — Click-Path (Mapped Before Screen Sketches)

This is the click-path the two upcoming screens (content warning, AI analysis summary) sit inside. The outer shape is client-confirmed from the kickoff demo and the sprint-review Q&A: a submitted case gets assigned to an auditor, the auditor passes through a warning gate before viewing flagged content, reviews it, and submits findings to the manager. Where the client left specifics open, this table marks the team's working design intention.

**Reading this as four flows:** the 9 stages below collapse into four flows that serve as a shared mental model for the wider team. This is a plain-language index into the same stages, not a replacement for them — use it for a quick shared picture, and the table below for the actual design detail and citations.
1. **Normal Review** — Stages 1–5: assigned → queue → consent gate → review → submission.
2. **High-Severity Cooldown** — Stages 6–9: submission triggers cooldown → cooldown screen → wellbeing check-in → return to queue.
3. **Decline** (branches off Stage 3) — routes straight to the manager; no SOS, no push alert.
4. **SOS Emergency** (available throughout Stage 4) — immediate stop, on-screen acknowledgement, manager alert/email, then follow-up.

### Cooldown Severity Model & Duration (Supporting Stages 6 and 9)

The exact severity trigger and cooldown duration were previously fully open — no client ruling and no proposal existed anywhere. The team has since adopted a sourced model as its working design intention; it is not yet client-confirmed, but it is no longer an unresolved question for the team.

| Level | Exposure severity | Examples | Cooldown |
|---|---|---|---|
| S1 – Low | Disturbing but limited impact | Verbal aggression, non-graphic conflict, staged/synthetic violence with no visible injury | None — normal review |
| S2 – Moderate | Realistic or sustained distress | Real physical conflict, visible minor injury, aggressive audio, repeated distressing content | 5 minutes, after sustained/repeated exposure (defined below) |
| S3 – High | Graphic or psychologically intense | Serious injury, significant blood, death aftermath, realistic self-harm, sexual violence themes | 15 minutes, mandatory |
| S4 – Critical | Extremely traumatic / specialist material | The most severe content categories the platform handles | 30 minutes minimum, plus a mandatory manager/support check-in and the option to stop or reassign the remaining shift |

**Baseline mechanism — scope-corrected 27 Aug 2026 per Hyuna (PM) feedback.** S1–S4 is mapped using simple rule-based logic over two existing AI outputs: the AI's own generated CVI score range and the AI's existing content tags/flag reasons (already shown at Stage 3) — not a separate manual classification step, and not a bespoke multi-factor scoring model. This correction exists because Naresh's confirmed AI scope is integrating existing AI services (per the kickoff demo, the vision/content analysis runs on watsonx.ai's Granite or Llama Maverick models) rather than building or fine-tuning custom models — and the project's own IBM x RMIT capstone brief describes exactly this split of labour: vision AI classifies the content, then a *"configurable rules engine"* (not a custom-trained model) turns that into a severity score. Computing S1–S4 from a six-weighted-factor formula, however well-sourced the factor list, would mean building that custom scoring logic ourselves — outside the confirmed scope. This is a mechanism correction, not a tier correction: the S1–S4 levels and cooldown durations above are untouched, it's the *how*, not the *what*. A minimum-severity floor still applies and still makes sense under this mechanism: specific tags already associated with the platform's most severe content categories can force a minimum S-level regardless of the CVI score or clip length — the floor is driven by tag presence, not by the (now-demoted) six-factor calculation, so it survives the correction unchanged.

**S1–S4 sits alongside the AI's existing severity/CVI rating, not in place of it.** The CVI score is the auditor's own case-assessment rating (confirmed, adjustable, submitted with the case); S1–S4 answers a different question — whether and how long a wellbeing cooldown applies. Keeping the two separate avoids overloading one score with two jobs and leaves the confirmed CVI mechanism untouched. The CVI range feeding the S1–S4 mapping above is specifically the AI's own auto-generated score at Stage 2 (queue view, where the severity tag has to already exist) — not the auditor's own adjusted CVI, which isn't submitted until Stage 5 and is too late to drive an S1–S4 tag the auditor needs to see before opening the case.

**"Sustained or repeated" for S2 is defined as:** continuous active exposure to S2-tagged content beyond roughly 2 minutes within a single case ("sustained"), or two or more S2-tagged cases reviewed within a single ~45–50 minute review block ("repeated") — that block length reuses the same micro-break window cited below rather than introducing a separate invented figure.

After an S3 or S4 case, the next case assigned should not itself be graphic. This is scoped deliberately narrow: a one-time filter excluding S3/S4-tagged cases from the single next assignment right after a cooldown, not a broader rule about spacing severe cases across a whole shift — the broader version of that question (Stage 1) stays open and is Dev/BA's to resolve, not decided here.

**Sourced from:** WorkSafe Victoria's Psychological Health Compliance Code (Appendix C — automated flagging, grayscale/thumbnails, rotating high-risk tasks, wellbeing checks), Safe Work Australia's Model Code of Practice on psychosocial hazards (minimising traumatic-material exposure, role rotation, recovery time), the Australian Classification Board's impact framework (realism, graphic detail, duration/repetition, context), Spence et al. (2024) and DeMarco, Watson & Norman (2026) on dose-response trauma exposure in content moderation, and Albulescu et al.'s (2022) microbreak meta-analysis on recovery-window length for highly demanding tasks.

**Still open:** the exact CVI-score-range cutoffs and which specific tags trigger the minimum-severity floor are a Dev/BA implementation detail — the mechanism (rule-based mapping) is decided, the exact numbers aren't. And per the source material itself, these exact minute values are a proposed risk-control policy informed by occupational-safety research, not an established clinical standard — stated as such rather than presented as settled science.

### Six-Factor Scoring — Nice-to-Have / Future Enhancement (Not Baseline)

The six-weighted-factor model originally proposed as the baseline severity-computation mechanism — content type, realism, graphic detail, duration/repetition, vulnerable context, and sensory intensity — is demoted to a Nice-to-Have / future-enhancement note, not deleted. It's kept here for traceability: if a future AI service the team *integrates* (rather than builds or fine-tunes) already outputs this level of structured factor detail as part of its own classification, this reasoning and its citations are directly reusable without redoing the research. It is explicitly **not** part of the Sprint 2 baseline — computing it ourselves today would mean building custom scoring logic, which sits outside Naresh's confirmed existing-AI-services-only scope (see the baseline mechanism note above).

Original reasoning, preserved unchanged: a short staged clip and a short real one shouldn't score the same, and a single violence flag doesn't capture that distinction — content type, realism, graphic detail, duration/repetition, vulnerable context, and sensory intensity were the proposed factors for making that distinction computable, drawing especially on the Australian Classification Board's impact framework (realism, graphic detail, duration/repetition, context), which maps closely onto this factor list. Same sourcing as the Cooldown Severity Model above. Feasibility of computing these six factors — via a future integrated classification service or otherwise — remains open, but only for this future-enhancement path, not for the corrected baseline above.

### Severity-Aware Assignment & Manual Skip (Supporting Stages 1–3)

The consecutive-severe-cases risk raised alongside the severity model above is addressed as three layers, each covering a different part of the same problem, not one fix:

1. **Assignment-side, soft preference (Stage 1).** The existing weighted round-robin (exposure level + case count) gains one more factor: an auditor who was recently assigned an S3/S4 case carries a short-lived "recent severity" flag, and the assignment logic prefers an unflagged auditor for the next S3/S4 case, even if it would otherwise be the flagged auditor's turn. This is a preference, not a hard block — if every available auditor is flagged (e.g. a queue surge), the system falls back to normal round-robin so cases don't sit unassigned. This is a small addition to the existing weighting logic, not a rebuild, and is proposed as something concrete for Dev to confirm feasibility on, rather than an open question with no shape.
2. **Post-cooldown, hard rule (Stage 6/9, Cooldown Severity Model above).** Already decided: the single case assigned immediately after an S3/S4 cooldown can't itself be S3/S4. Guaranteed, not just preferred, since this is the one moment the risk is both highest and known for certain.
3. **Auditor-side, manual skip (Stages 2–3).** The existing Decline mechanism (Stage 3) currently only triggers after opening a case and reaching the warning gate. It's extended here to also be triggerable directly from the queue view (Stage 2), using the severity tag already shown there, before opening — same non-judgment framing, same manager routing as the existing decline path (Research Note 1), just a second, earlier entry point rather than a separate mechanism to learn. This is the backstop for whatever the first two layers don't catch.

None of the three layers alone is sufficient — Layer 1 reduces how often this happens system-wide, Layer 2 guarantees the one highest-risk moment, Layer 3 gives the auditor a direct out for anything that still gets through. **Still open:** the exact recency window and tie-breaking behaviour for Layer 1's "recent severity" flag (e.g. last N cases vs. last T minutes) is an implementation detail for Dev to confirm, not decided here.

**Decline & queue-skip reason list — adopted 27 Aug 2026.** Both Layer 3 above and Stage 3's warning-gate decline share the same reason categories, in order: **Content more severe than AI indicated** (surfaced first — the most operationally useful signal for spotting AI mis-tagging patterns, and the least emotionally loaded to click), **Near my exposure limit** (plain-language relabel of the earlier "Exposure Buffer" draft), **Personal Trigger**, and **Other** (with free-text comment). The queue-level skip (Layer 3) carries its own distinct reason value — "skipped based on visible severity, not opened" — so pattern-review can tell the two entry points apart later. None of the four options is pre-selected by default: research on default-selection bias finds roughly 60% of users read a pre-set option as the implicit "recommended" choice even when it's never labelled that way, so a pre-filled reason on a decline form would quietly steer auditors toward a reason that isn't necessarily theirs — exactly the kind of soft nudge Marielle Lee's own dark-pattern gut-check ("does the user have real freedom to choose?", Q7) is meant to catch.

### AI/STT Failure Handling (Supporting Stage 4)

Not previously described anywhere in this note or any other source document — adopted 27 Aug 2026, using design authority the client explicitly delegated (Naresh declined to specify fallback behaviour directly at the sprint review: "team should treat this as open/self-defined requirement").

If AI or Speech-to-Text processing fails or can't complete for a case: visual exposure defaults to **maximum blur**, not whatever the AI would otherwise have suggested — unknown severity maps to the most protective state, not a neutral one. The AI-summary panel is replaced with a distinct failure state, not a small badge easily missed — e.g. "AI analysis could not be completed for this video — severity score and incident timeline are unavailable. Review with extra caution." Before any raw content is shown, the auditor makes a deliberate choice — proceed with reduced AI support, or decline — rather than being silently routed into a degraded review.

If processing fails **mid-review**, after the auditor has already started (not caught at pre-screen): this is treated the same as an unexpected-exposure event — logged, wellbeing protections applied, manager notified — since a mid-review AI/STT failure carries the same risk profile as an AI mis-tag: the auditor may be looking at content with no severity information to prepare them.

### Exposure Clock — What Counts (Supporting Stage 4)

The counting rule referenced in Stage 4 above is adopted 27 Aug 2026, backed by real evidence rather than an internal guess: **active source-video playback counts toward the daily exposure total regardless of blur/grayscale/mute state; paused video and AI-summary-only viewing do not count; replaying a clip counts again, not once.** A 2026 experimental study found blur and grayscale did not significantly reduce immediate anxiety, negative affect, or intrusive-image effects compared with unmodified content — so counting blurred/muted time as real exposure is measuring the right thing, not being overly cautious for its own sake; the controls remain valuable for reviewer autonomy, they just shouldn't be assumed to reduce exposure risk. Replays counting again follows the same logic: re-exposure is still exposure, even if it feels repetitive to the auditor in the moment.

Stage 3's exact pop-up copy, Stage 4's SOS/wellbeing-check-in interactions, and Stages 6–9's cooldown experience are genuinely open design space; each team-designed addition above is traceable to a specific research note or Marielle Lee recommendation (cited inline), not invented from scratch — treat this table as the team's working design intention for the two upcoming screens plus the cooldown addition, not something awaiting outside confirmation. Stages 6 and 9's trigger threshold and duration are a different kind of open, though: no client ruling exists for either — the Cooldown Severity Model above is now the team's settled working design, not a confirmation from Naresh or Emily. Assignment weighting's consecutive-severity risk (Stage 1) now has a proposed design too — see Severity-Aware Assignment & Manual Skip above — with only the implementation detail (the recency window) left for Dev to confirm.

## 2. Auditor's Likely Background/Context (Brief Note)

An auditor works through a queue of AI-pre-processed cases against a daily, time-measured exposure cap, with blur-by-default and a mandatory cooldown after high-severity exposure (shaped in Stages 6–9 below) — they can also decline a case, or trigger SOS if the AI under-tags something and they're unexpectedly exposed. Per our Week 1 auditor research and the three dedicated Auditor persona variants, this isn't one fixed relationship to those protections but three postures across a tenure spectrum: still forming first impressions, with no track record to judge the protections by (Jules, 3 weeks in, no prior moderation experience); competent, but not yet confident that declining or SOS carries no real professional cost (Maya, ~3 years, the client brief's own named reviewer); and having largely resolved that trust, but sharpened instead into doubt over whether the exposure clock itself is accurate (Theo, ~9 years). For Jules and Maya, that's why Stage 3's decline path carries its own non-judgment reassurance and Stage 4's SOS needs visible human acknowledgement, not just a background email — the same trust gap the Manager Assumptions Note's Hypothesis 1 closes from the manager's side. For Theo, it's why Stage 4's exposure-clock breakdown matters more than reassurance ever would. No real auditors have been interviewed for this project, so this spectrum, like the rest of the Auditor persona set, is a team-defined working hypothesis rather than a confirmed profile.
